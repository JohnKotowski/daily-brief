Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Mini, an open-weights model that runs on a laptop Thinking Machines · HN 993About 20GB of memory, and by the lab's numbers the best coding scores of any open model under 100B parameters.

The model has 26.4 billion parameters, of which 3.4 billion are active per token, and runs its middle layers three times for each token. Thinking Machines says this lets it reason more without writing longer chains of thought, and reports 48.2% on DeepSWE v1.1, ahead of every open-weights model below 100B parameters. Factual recall is no better than other models its size, since that depends on parameter count. The company's researchers ran it offline on a MacBook Pro during a flight. Weights are open, and it is available on the company's Tinker service. All figures are the lab's own.

Signal blocks screenshots by default to keep AI agents out of private chats Slashdot · ReutersSignal calls operating-system agents an "existential threat" to what it promises users.

Signal says assistants built into operating systems can read what is on screen, which defeats end-to-end encryption at the last step. The new protection is on by default; users get a pop-up with the option to disable it when Signal detects a screenshot or an assistant trying to read the screen. Signal calls the measure "incomplete", says it cannot offer it reliably on macOS, and asks Apple, Google and Microsoft to give developers a proper way to refuse agent access and to document what their agents read. It did the same against Microsoft's Recall last year.

Paper: a 27B open model edits its own weights and scores 6.6 points higher on unseen problems arXiv · HN 408No gradients and no training run; the authors call it a step toward recursive self-improvement.

The authors gave the model access to its own weights, its graded answers and the ability to run itself. It diagnosed its failures, rewrote the weights responsible, tested each edit on practice problems and kept only those that worked; accuracy rose across eight consecutive sessions. In a second experiment it restored a damaged copy of itself to more than 80% of the original accuracy on image tasks without seeing the original weights. Smaller models in the same family made no edit or broke themselves. This is a preprint from Nov 16, revised Nov 18, and I have read only the abstract; the claims are the authors' and are not peer reviewed.

Latent Space: formal verification as the answer to AI-generated "slop" in code Latent SpaceThe same Lean tooling behind this week's maths claims is being sold as a way to prove software correct.

The piece surveys Harmonic, Theorem, Axiom Math and a newcomer called &Truth, and interviews &Truth co-founder Clark Barrett of Stanford, co-creator of the cvc5 solver, which he says Amazon now calls billions of times a day. His distinction: tests and scanners find many problems, but you never know whether you found all of them, while a proof tells you when you are done. &Truth's first target is memory safety in the world's C code. Verification costs a lot of tokens today; Anthropic reported Opus 5.5 reaching a 50% solve rate on 36 small verification tasks with about 40% fewer tokens than Opus 5.
