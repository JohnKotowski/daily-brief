Thompson's "trusting trust" attack works through GNU strip — one tampered binary backdoored nearly every binary in a full NixOS installerarXiv · HN 207 The 1984 attack was thought to need a compiler. This paper does it with a utility that never sees source code, only finished ELF files.

Ken Thompson's Turing lecture showed a compiler could insert a backdoor into itself and into everything it built, invisibly. Malka, Sharma, Monperrus, Zacchiroli and Zimmermann show the compiler was never the point: GNU strip, which only manipulates finished binaries, is enough. Tamper with the copy in NixOS's bootstrap seed and the payload propagates through the entire build, backdooring almost every binary in a real graphical installer built from a real nixpkgs revision, with no detection. The implication for reproducible-build efforts is uncomfortable — bit-for-bit reproducibility verifies the build, not the seed. Related weekend read: a Canadian engineer factored two 512-bit root-CA keys from Netscape 4.51 on a Ryzen desktop in about 30 hours each; those keys expired decades ago, but it is the same lesson about what "trusted" meant in the 90s.

Google Maps deliberately sent some drivers the long way in 10 US cities — and kept the improvement small on purposeSFGate · Nature Cities · via Slashdot Speeds up 2% on average, trips 0.7% shorter, LA highways up 4.6%, ~1,000 t CO₂ a year saved. Co-author: "if you are too successful, then more people will start driving."

A Google research team used Maps routing to nudge a small share of drivers onto slightly longer paths in San Francisco, Los Angeles, Seattle, Salt Lake City, Chicago, Atlanta and four other cities, spreading load across the network. The measured effect is modest by design: average speeds up 2%, travel times down 0.7%, with LA highways seeing 4.6% gains. The interesting part is the restraint — the team argues that a visible improvement triggers induced demand, more people drive, and the gain evaporates, so the optimum is an improvement drivers cannot feel. It is the cleanest real-world statement of the "system optimum vs user optimum" problem, and it lands the same week a few hundred thousand Maps users were quietly A/B-tested into a longer commute for the greater good.
