Lenny's: Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar on advanced evals — most AI teams skip "error discovery" and jump to metrics, so they measure the wrong failures Lenny's Newsletter · guest postA three-step, human-in-the-loop process: find failures by reading traces, annotate and cluster them into failure modes, then automate measurement. Avoids "criteria drift".

The most useful piece in Lenny's this week isn't the model bake-off, it's this. Husain and Shankar's argument is that teams write eval metrics before they know what actually goes wrong in their product, so the metrics encode guesses. Their process: pull real traces, read them (yes, by hand, with a free plugin and a coding agent to speed it up), annotate every failure in open-ended notes, cluster the notes into named failure modes, and only then decide which modes deserve an automated judge. The payoff is that your evals track failures users hit rather than failures you imagined — and it stops the "criteria drift" where the rubric quietly changes every time someone reruns it.

Why it matters to you: this is the missing layer for the inbox sweep and the daily brief — you've been correcting failures by memory-file, which is step two without step one. A weekly trace-read of the sweep's dropped/kept decisions would give you the failure-mode list to write evals against.

Every: Mike Taylor's "good writing with AI starts before the prompt" — a 1,200-word piece drops from a full day to under two hours, and the part that shrinks is the drafting, not the thinking Every · Also True for HumansGather sources, record unstructured thoughts, outline, feed the context to several models, then refine by hand. The voice survives because the ideas were yours before the model saw them.

Taylor's workflow is the opposite of "write me an article about X". He front-loads the work that models can't do for him — collecting sources, dictating messy first thoughts, building an outline that's actually an argument — and only then hands that pile to multiple models, compares drafts, and edits the winner by hand. The claim is that this keeps his voice because the structure and opinions were fixed before generation; the model is doing the typing, not the thinking. His time budget is the interesting part: discovery and editing didn't shrink at all, drafting went to near zero.
