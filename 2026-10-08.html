Innovation & Infra

Francis Halzen wins the 2026 Nobel in Physics, alone, for IceCube — a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice that traced high-energy neutrinos back to black holes Slashdot Cosmic rays were discovered in 1912 and nobody could say where they came from; IceCube gave the first evidence of a source and started multi-messenger astronomy.

Halzen (UW–Madison) is the sole laureate. IceCube's thousands of light sensors sit about a mile under the South Pole ice, watching for the flash when a near-massless neutrino finally hits something; because neutrinos travel undeflected, they point straight back at their source, which charged cosmic rays never could. The detector traced them to supermassive black holes and distant galaxies. "IceCube is like no other telescope in the world," said interim chancellor Eric Wilcots — the prize is as much for the thirty-year engineering bet as the physics.
