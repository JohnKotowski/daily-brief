Claude cracks the 26-year NIST curve mystery: the ECDSA seeds the NSA handed over in 2000 were not a hashed secret passphrase, they were lrand48 output seeded with Unix timestamps Anthropic · HN 858 pts Decades of backdoor speculation, a bounty and a Koblitz–Menezes paper, closed in under half an hour by an internal research model that was not pointed at the popular theory.

The seed values behind the NIST P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 curves were supplied by the NSA in 2000 with no explanation, and their provenance became a standing question in cryptography — Koblitz and Menezes wrote "A Riddle Wrapped in an Enigma" about it, a bounty sat unclaimed, and the leading theory was that the late NSA cryptographer Jerry Solinas had hashed a humorous phrase. In October an Anthropic employee asked an internal research model to work it out without that hint. Claude found that none of the seeds were hashes at all: they are the output of the C library's lrand48 random-number generator seeded with a Unix timestamp, which reproduces every one. Anthropic frames it as a tidy closing chapter rather than a security event — the curves were already trusted and nothing changes for users — but it ends 26 years of suspicion that the constants hid a trapdoor, and it is a clean example of a model doing genuine open-ended research rather than retrieval.

Inside Claude Code's review pipeline: with Claude writing ~90% of its own code, merged PRs doubled, reverts fell 40%, and Anthropic's monorepo now lands 30,000 changes a day claude.dev · HN 340 pts The human gate at the end of the road is replaced by layered agents — a fresh-context reviewer before the PR, parallel bug hunters with verifiers voting, and a risk classifier that sets zero, one or two human approvers.

The Claude Code team describes how review changed once most of the code was model-written. Before a PR exists, a second agent reviews the diff in a fresh context. On every PR, parallel bug-hunting agents propose findings and separate verifier agents vote on them, a dedicated security pass runs, and a risk classifier decides whether the change needs zero, one or two human approvers. Anthropic's monorepo went from about 2,500 changes a day in May to more than 30,000 in September; from June to September, merged Claude Code PRs more than doubled while the revert share fell by over 40%. The metric they optimise is minimum human review time, with reverts and incidents as the guardrail. For a two-person engineering team this is the practical template: one fresh-context review agent before you open the PR costs almost nothing and catches most of what a tired second reader would.

Weekend read — "You could have invented transformers from memory": a linear layer stores about 64 memories, an MLP about 2,700, attention over a million Alex Wang · HN 288 pts One quantity, the interference-to-signal ratio, explains why each architecture step buys so much recall. Derivation-heavy, worth the half hour.

Alex Wang reframes linear maps, MLPs and self-attention as successively better associative-memory stores and uses a single number — the ratio of interference to signal when you query a stored key — to define each one's capacity. With 64-dimensional keys the capacities come out at roughly 64 memories for a linear layer, about 2,700 for an MLP, and more than a million for attention, which is the cleanest intuition on offer for why transformers can recall so much from so little. It builds up from first principles, so the title's claim is almost fair: follow the capacity argument and attention is the thing you would have designed next.
