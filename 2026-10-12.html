500 billion tokens later: fleets of agents decompiled a complete first-person shooter — 99% of functions recovered, 83% byte-exact, and the game runs momo5502.com via Hacker News · 197 pts A three-month, roughly 600–700B-token project that is the clearest public benchmark yet of what long-running agent swarms can grind through when the output is machine-verifiable.

Maurice Heumann ran fleets of agents — mainly Claude Sonnet 5 and Opus 5.5, Codex, and in the final weeks about 14 "Luna" agents plus two Opus 5.5 — against the binary of an unnamed popular FPS and had them reconstruct source. Result: 99% of functions recovered, 83% byte-exact to the original, and a build that plays without noticeable bugs. Total spend was 600–700 billion tokens, around 300B of it on Luna; no dollar figure is given. The reason it worked is the same reason the math-proof stories worked last week — every step is checkable by a compiler and a byte diff, so the agents could run unattended for weeks. Read it as the ceiling for verifiable-output work, not for the fuzzy kind.

"I'm sorry, but you still have to think" — DHH's agent-rewritten Campfire delivered 1% of notifications under load, and the fix pushed max latency past two minutes itsallaboutthebit.com via Hacker News · 375 pts Piotr Sarnacki's post-mortem on vague prompts: the Rust rewrite silently dropped CSRF tokens and Redis, and nobody noticed until a closed-loop benchmark.

DHH had agents rewrite Campfire from Rails into Rust, Elixir and Go as a demonstration. Sarnacki benchmarked the results and found the Rust version delivered only 1% of chat notifications at 100 requests per second; raising the broadcast channel capacity from 256 to 16,384 got delivery to about 90% but blew max latency out past 130 seconds. Along the way the rewrite had quietly removed CSRF protection and the Redis layer because the prompt never said to keep them. His conclusion is not anti-AI: codegen is fine, but someone still has to own the trade-offs, failure modes and system bounds, because the model will pick defaults wherever the spec is silent. Pairs with the decompilation story above — the difference is whether the output is verifiable.

Sakana AI + UC Berkeley: MASS, a recursive self-improvement loop where one model is solver, workflow optimiser and judge — win rate on held-out research tasks up from 54% to 70% in two rounds Sakana AI via Hacker News · 113 pts Starts from Qwen3.6-27B, searches for better multi-agent team layouts using the model's own evaluations, then fine-tunes on the winners. Multi-Agent Self-Supervision runs an inner loop that searches over multi-agent workflows using the model's own judgments as the signal, and an outer loop that fine-tunes the base model on the best runs. "Passkeys are a trap" — the argument that WebAuthn's remote attestation hands platforms a lock-in dial, and that TOTP or a hardware key is the safer default Non-Functional via Hacker News · 592 pts The weekend's most-upvoted post. The claim is not that passkeys are insecure; it is that the spec lets sites reject passkeys from software the platform has not blessed.

The author's case: "passkey" is a brand covering many optional WebAuthn variants, the FIDO Alliance has no consumer advocates on it, and the dangerous option is attestation — a site can demand proof that your passkey lives in Apple's, Google's or Microsoft's store and refuse ones from Linux, a custom Android build or an open-source password manager. That turns an authentication standard into a distribution-control lever, and the post cites Google's removal of uBlock Origin from Chrome as evidence platforms use such levers when they have them. Recommendation: TOTP codes or a YubiKey-style hardware key. The HN counter-view is that almost no consumer site enforces attestation today. Both are true; the question is who gets to flip the switch later.

Let's Encrypt cuts certificate lifetime from 90 to 64 days from Feb 10, 2027, on the way to 45 days in 2028 — 64-day certs hit staging this Wednesday Linuxiac via Slashdot · Oct 12 If anything you run still renews certificates by hand or by a monthly cron, it breaks in February. Domain-authorization reuse also drops from 30 days to 10, then to seven hours.

Certificates issued or renewed from Feb 10, 2027 get a 64-day default; the last 90-day certificate expires May 11, 2027; nothing is revoked early. The 64-day certs land in the staging environment on Oct 14, 2026 so automation can be tested now. This is the intermediate step toward a 45-day default in 2028, and the authorization-reuse window shrinks on the same schedule — 30 days to 10 days now, to seven hours in 2028. Anything on certbot or Caddy with auto-renew is unaffected; the people who get hurt are the ones with a 60-day renewal cron or a manual process. Worth a two-minute check across the PricingSaaS, PuckFinder and Buyerson hosts.
