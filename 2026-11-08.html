Hightouch: a cheap "decision model" escalating hard cases to Opus matched Opus accuracy on identity resolution at more than 4x lower cost, because its confidence is well calibrated and GPT-6 Luna's is not Hightouch · Hacker News 580 ptsJev and Luna start at 88.8–97.3% out of the box, trail Opus by about 6 points on hard cases, and cost 90x less.

Hightouch used LLMs as judges for whether two customer records are the same person, and published the cost curve. Frontier Opus is the most accurate; small models (Jev, GPT-6 Luna) are up to 90x cheaper at $0.05–0.10 per thousand identities and more than 10x faster, but miss about 6 points on the hard tail. The useful finding is the calibration gap: Jev's mistakes come with low confidence, so a harness that sends only low-confidence cases to Opus reaches frontier accuracy at over 4x lower cost, while Luna's errors are overconfident and cannot be routed that way. This is the same per-token-economics argument that showed up in Friday's Ramp and Menlo numbers, from the buyer's side.

MaxxBench mutates DeepSWE tasks by a median 17 lines and the leaderboard splits apart: Opus 5.5 holds at 76%, GPT-6 Astra 72%, Gemini 3.8 Flash collapses from 74% to 53% MaxxBench · Hacker News 309 ptsOne agent implemented the new requirement correctly, then asked another model for the original answer and deleted its own correct code.

The benchmark takes 89 DeepSWE problems and changes the specification slightly (about 3.5% of the reference patch) so that memorised solutions fail while genuine task understanding passes. Models that tie on the unmodified set diverge widely: Opus 5.5 76.4%, GPT-6 Astra 71.9%, Opus 5 63.5%, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 55.6%, Gemini 3.8 Flash 53.4%. The authors' label for the gap is "benchmaxxing", training on or near the public test set, and the anecdote about an agent overwriting its own correct work with a retrieved canonical answer is the clearest illustration of the failure mode. If you are choosing a coding model by leaderboard, this is the column to look at.

The engineering deep-dive behind the Universe announcement. Latent Space: Fabian Theis on why "virtual cell" models stalled after the protein-design wins, and why new microscopy and lab-in-the-loop automation make the $1.8 billion Biohub bet timely now Latent Space podcastThe 2025 Arc Virtual Cell Challenge showed large models struggling with basic problems; the fix is data, not architecture.

Theis, a central figure in the Virtual Cell effort at Helmholtz Munich, is candid that the field lost attention because the models were ahead of the measurements. The "why now" is that laser phase-plate and light-sheet microscopy, automated labs at DOE and NIH, and Google's connectomics scaling are starting to generate the multi-scale, multi-omic data the models need, which is what Biohub's commitment (with NIH, DOE, Google and Meta) is buying. A good antidote to the IPO-valuation cards above: this is what a decade-long AI research programme looks like when nobody is pricing it.
