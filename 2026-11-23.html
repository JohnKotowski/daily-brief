OpenAI's chief scientist proposes perplexity as the shared yardstick for limits on AI progress OpenAI · HN 587 · TechCrunchOpenAI published its internal pretraining measurements, from GPT-3.5 to models below a loss of 0.8.

Jakub Pachocki argues that capability benchmarks go out of date quickly, while perplexity, how well a model predicts the next token on held-out data, has stayed informative for years. He proposes it as a metric to compare developers and to anchor shared safety standards, and publishes OpenAI's own numbers as a first step. He writes that GPT-4 sat at about 0.91 and that below 0.8 "we have seen the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics". TechCrunch places the post in the debate that followed Dario Amodei's plan to "pace the frontier": companies would agree not to pass a threshold until their ability to understand and monitor model behaviour reaches a defined milestone. TechCrunch reports that Sam Altman called the post the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while.

Tim O'Reilly: "What Happened to Silicon Valley?" O'Reilly Radar · HN 649A lecture on how an industry that created more value than it captured turned to extraction.

This is the text of a lecture given in Margaret Levi's name. O'Reilly starts from his own company, built from $500 with no venture capital on the principle "create more value than you capture", and from the early web as a system that matched people with what others had made. He distinguishes rents earned by innovating from what he calls robber baron rents, collected through market power once growth slows, and treats the move from one to the other as close to a law of the technology cycle. He criticises hype as a tool for drawing investment and the pursuit of monopoly as the standard pitch. On AI he is more hopeful: he sees the agentic web taking the shape of open source, where people bring their own models and infrastructure, and expects the lasting business models to arrive in a second stage, as pay-per-click did years after the web itself.

Python Software Foundation documents how to block the risky GitHub Actions triggers across an organisation Slashdot · Alpha-Omegapull_request_target and issue_comment can let a stranger run code with your repository's secrets.

Seth Larson and Mike Fiedler removed these triggers across the psf, python and pypi organisations and wrote up how to do the same. The safe pull_request trigger runs fork code with a read-only token and no secrets. pull_request_target looks almost identical in a workflow file but runs with a read/write token and access to secrets, so a workflow that checks out and runs the pull request hands a stranger your credentials. issue_comment carries the same risk when comment text reaches a shell command. On a GitHub Team or Enterprise plan one policy turns them off for every repository; on a Free plan it is one repository at a time. Worth a check on the pricingsaas organisation, where agent workflows run with real secrets.
