Anthropic: a "global workspace" inside language models Anthropic · HN 364 New interpretability work finds a shared internal scratchpad where a model integrates information across tasks — echoing a leading theory of human consciousness.

Anthropic's research team reports evidence of a "global workspace" in large models — an internal representation where information from different subsystems is broadcast and integrated, rather than staying siloed in task-specific circuits. The name is a deliberate nod to Global Workspace Theory in cognitive science, one of the dominant accounts of how the brain routes information into conscious awareness. The practical payoff isn't philosophical: if there's a central bottleneck where the model "decides" what to attend to, that's a high-leverage place to steer behaviour, debug failures, and build safety interventions. Read it as another brick in the interpretability wall Anthropic keeps arguing is the real moat.

Ternlight: a 7 MB embedding model that runs entirely in the browser HN 214 A WASM-compiled embedding model small enough to ship in a web page — semantic search with zero server round-trips.

Ternlight is a tiny (7 MB) text-embedding model compiled to WebAssembly, so it runs client-side with no API calls and no server. The demo does live semantic search in the browser tab. The interesting trend here isn't the model itself — it's that "embeddings as a local primitive" is arriving: once a usable embedder fits in the size of a hero image, semantic search, dedup, and RAG retrieval can happen on-device, offline, and free of per-query cost. For anyone building search or "chat with your docs" features, this is a nudge to reconsider whether you need a vector-DB vendor at all for small corpora.

GLM 5.2 and the coming AI margin collapse martinalderson.com · HN 393 An open-weight model near the frontier at a fraction of the price forces the question: how long can anyone hold inference margins?

Martin Alderson uses the release of GLM 5.2 — a competitive open-weight model priced far below the US frontier labs — to argue that AI inference is heading for a margin collapse similar to what commoditised cloud compute. His point: when a "good enough" model is available at a tenth of the price and can be self-hosted, the pricing power of the premium labs erodes fast, and the economics start to look like a race to the bottom on tokens. The counter-argument (frontier capability keeps moving, enterprises pay for reliability and safety) is real, but this is the clearest recent write-up of the bear case on AI unit economics. Pair with the Vercel "models are swappable" interview above.
