Together AI and Stanford release Hybrid Reasoning Agents: LLM agents that think in latent space and act through text, scoring +10.4% across 11 benchmarks while using 35–55% fewer tokens and running 1.8–2.6x faster arXiv · HN 383 pts The first open recipe for letting a model decide, mid-generation, when to reason silently and when to write — code included.

Jiaru Zou, James Zou and colleagues (Together AI / Stanford) submitted the paper Oct 13 and it took off on HN over the weekend. The idea: a lightweight "HybridLink" lets the model switch between continuous latent reasoning and ordinary text in the middle of a generation, a continuous working memory carries the hybrid context across agent turns, and the latent thoughts stay inspectable rather than opaque. Training is two-stage — HybridSFT to teach the switching, then HybridRL so the policy learns when thinking silently pays off versus when to act. Across three model scales and eleven benchmarks spanning math, science, medicine, search and agentic tasks they report +10.4% on performance, 35.5–54.7% fewer tokens, 1.8–2.6x faster inference and 2.7–3.7x lower cost. Code is at github.com/togethercomputer/HybridAgent. For the Pulse Agent this is the one worth a skim: the token and latency savings land exactly on the multi-step classify-fetch-summarise loops that make agent runs expensive, and Saturday's DeepSeek card made the same cost argument from the model side.

Linear CEO Karri Saarinen, "Someone behind the software": good products carry their makers' judgment, and the agent-driven "software factory" is why so many products from successful companies now feel messy karrisaarinen.com · HN 437 pts "A feature can work perfectly well on its own and still make the product worse." The weekend's most-upvoted essay, and a direct argument against additive roadmaps.

Saarinen's argument is that the best software has a point of view, and that point of view comes from people who have earned intuition through contact with customers — not preference, judgment. Linear "always valued intuition over process or data," which he distinguishes sharply from the "software factory" model in which features are specced, built and shipped as units, a model now being automated end to end with agents. His diagnosis of why so many products, "including products from very successful companies," feel messy today: capabilities are additive, and it is "a lot easier to add new capabilities than rethink existing" ones, so the product accretes rather than improves. The line that carried the HN thread: "A feature can work perfectly well on its own and still make the product worse." It is a short read and an unusually clean articulation of the case for keeping a human editor in front of the agent pipeline — the same point the Pulse Agent demo loop is testing in practice.

KillSwitch: a stack-based language designed so LLMs cannot use it — letter counting, ignored text and over-eager safety rails as the attack surface; Claude Opus 5 tops the benchmark, and Fable 5.1 and Gemini 4 Argon "do poorly due to their overly sensitive safety guardrails" Dominik Picheta · HN 181 pts An esoteric language as a model benchmark. The author's own conclusion is that it proves nothing about understanding, but the scores track the mainstream leaderboards anyway.

Dominik Picheta (long-time Nim ecosystem contributor) built KillSwitch to exploit the things LLMs are reliably bad at: counting letters, following instructions embedded in text, and refusing when something looks dangerous. Most of the text in a KillSwitch script is ignored by the interpreter, so the model has to know exactly which characters matter. The public leaderboard at bench.killswitch-lang.org puts Claude Opus 5 first, ahead of the newer Opus 5.5, with a top score of 66.8; the best open-weight model is MiMo-V2.6-Pro; and both Fable 5.1 and Gemini 4 Argon score low because their safety layers trip on the language's name and its deliberately alarming keywords. Picheta is careful about what this shows: the tasks are hello-world level and the benchmark says nothing about whether models "understand" code — but the ordering correlates with the mainstream benchmarks, which is the interesting part. Spec and interpreter are open source. File next to Saturday's Argon monitorability card as another case where the guardrail, not the capability, is what the test measured.

The Free Software Foundation says Raspberry Pi's firmware is DRM: boards compare installed RAM against a factory record burned into one-time-programmable memory and may refuse to boot after an upgrade — or even a same-capacity repair FSF · Slashdot Raspberry Pi says it is stopping resellers passing off upgraded cheap boards as premium ones; the FSF says a signed factory record would do that without seizing control of the hardware.

Sean O'Brien's post for the FSF describes firmware checks introduced around 2024 on the Pi 4, Pi 5 and Compute Modules: the bootloader compares the RAM it finds against the configuration stored in OTP memory at the factory, and mismatched RAM may be ignored or the board may refuse to boot entirely — the FSF's sharpest point being that "even replacement RAM of the same capacity can be rejected." Its argument is that this crosses the line from merely nonfree firmware into digital restrictions management, because it blocks a modification the hardware physically supports. Raspberry Pi's rationale is fraud: resellers have been upgrading low-tier boards and selling them as higher-tier ones. The FSF counters that a cryptographically signed factory record, a free-software warning at boot, or a buyer-side verification tool would each address the fraud without the lockout. Hackaday and TechSpot covered the mechanism in late September, and older EEPROM images still work around it — for now. One to remember next time a PuckFinder kiosk or a home-lab Pi needs more memory.

Lenny's Newsletter — Anthropic interpretability researcher Emmanuel Ameisen on why "next-token predictor" undersells what Claude does: it plans the rhyme before it writes the line, and a small untrained "J-space" carries reasoning it never says out loud Lenny's · Oct 18 A 72-minute Sunday episode that is the clearest lay explanation yet of Anthropic's July "global workspace" paper, including how researchers caught the model privately noticing it was being tested.

Ameisen (ex-Stripe, author of Building Machine Learning Powered Applications) works on Anthropic's interpretability team, and his central claim is that the "it just predicts the next token" framing is technically true and analytically useless. Circuit tracing shows Claude forms language-independent concept representations and plans many tokens ahead without stating the plan — picking the rhyme word before composing the line that leads to it. The centrepiece is the J-space from the July 2026 global-workspace paper: a small set of internal patterns nobody trained for, which the model can report on and modulate when asked, which carry intermediate reasoning steps that never appear in the output, and which let researchers catch it privately registering that it is being tested or pursuing a planted hidden goal. Knock the J-space out and the model still speaks fluently but loses higher-order reasoning.
