Vermont's largest power source is now a "power plant" spread across thousands of home batteries — a virtual plant that keeps the lights on through storms and is cheaper than the gas peakers it replaces BBC Future · HN 186 ptsGreen Mountain Power leases Powerwalls to homeowners for a monthly fee and dispatches them at peak. Owners get outage protection; the utility avoids building generation. Rural customers who were about to buy gas generators are the poster case.

The interesting number isn't the battery count, it's the business model: the utility owns the dispatch rights and the homeowner owns the resilience, and both would rather have that than a generator in the garage. Ontario has the same storm profile and the same rural-outage problem; Hydro Ottawa runs pilots but nothing at this scale. It's also the first real-world answer to the "AI buildout doubles residential electricity demand" line from Monday's Brookings item — distributed storage is how a grid absorbs that without new plants.

How Delhi cut electricity losses from 50% to 5% — a power-systems professor who lived through the daily blackouts of 2002 explains the twenty-year fix: privatised distribution, metering, and accountability IEEE Spectrum · HN 515 ptsIn the early 2000s more than half the city's power vanished to theft, obsolete wiring and utilities with no customer service. The turnaround is the largest urban grid fix on record. 13-minute read.

A first-person engineering history rather than a policy paper, which is why it hit 515 points. The lesson generalises: the technology (smart meters, SCADA, aerial bundled cable so you can't tap the line) was available the whole time; what changed was who bore the cost of losses. Once the distribution companies owned the shortfall, the metering paid for itself. Any "why doesn't X just fix Y" infrastructure question usually has the same answer.
