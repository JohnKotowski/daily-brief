Stanford shows bleached coral fully recovers within weeks when touching a healthy fragment of the same genet: the symbiont population doubled every four days, while fragments a few millimetres apart died Nature · HN 440 pts It started as a lab accident: colour "seeping" into a bleached branch where it touched a healthy one.

Published in Science Advances on Oct 14, the finding began when Stephen Palumbi at Hopkins Marine Station noticed colour seeping into a bleached Pocillopora branch where it touched a healthy one. PhD student Danny Collins set up ten pairs; the five touching pairs recovered in eight weeks, the five separated by millimetres died at 20 weeks. Microscopy shows the fragments fuse via a tissue bridge that ferries algal symbionts across; after one week of contact a bleached branch can finish recovering on its own. The catch for restoration is that fusion only happens between fragments of the same genetic individual, not across species or even other individuals of the same species. Co-author Fiorenza Micheli: "fast and full recovery is possible in bleached coral" and this "can inspire solutions that eventually may be scalable."

Columbia chemists find why microplastics inflame tissue: the water at their hydrophobic surface turns chemically reactive, and shredding a credit card into 10-micron particles multiplies that surface area 200-fold Slashdot The first mechanism behind a decade of correlations with heart attack, stroke and dementia.

Microplastics have been correlated with heart attack, stroke, dementia and brain tumours without anyone showing how. A Nature paper from Wei Min's lab tested polystyrene, polyethylene, PVC, polypropylene and PET and found that water molecules at the plastic interface cannot form their normal hydrogen bonds, which shifts the local electrical environment and generates highly reactive species that modify nearby proteins and cell membranes. Bulk plastic's presumed inertness "overlooks the toxicity of their fragmented microscale forms," Min says. It is a candidate explanation for the inflammation, oxidative stress and reduced mitochondrial activity seen in earlier studies, though the team is clear the link to actual tissue damage in humans is still open.

Weekend read: Jake Archibald benchmarks JPEG XL against AVIF now that JXL is in every major browser, and AVIF is 15–25% smaller on photos, 30–90% smaller on screenshots, and 2–3x faster to decode jakearchibald.com · HN 320 pts The first rigorous head-to-head since Chrome 155 shipped JXL: its arrival does not dethrone AVIF for the web.

For lossy web photos the best JXL is typically 15–25% larger than AVIF at default effort and 6–20% at maximum, with occasional outliers where JXL wins. The newer jixel encoder beats libjxl except on detailed transparency, where it encodes losslessly and balloons files to 2–3x AVIF. For screenshots and illustrations AVIF is "much better," and it is faster to encode per byte and 2–3x faster to decode at medium quality. Practical takeaways: higher effort settings pay off for AVIF and jixel but not libjxl, and AVIF's 4:2:0 mode should not be the default. For anyone shipping product screenshots at scale, the answer is still AVIF.

Latent Space — "Lab-in-the-Loop at Compute Speed": Leash Bio put about 4 billion binding measurements in front of about 2,000 Kaggle teams and nobody beat chance on an unseen chemical library Latent Space · Oct 14 The sobering number for anyone betting on "AI solves chemistry," plus a Clever Hans finding: who made the molecule predicts activity about as well as its structure.

Ian Quigley's Leash Bio runs DNA-encoded libraries, a notoriously noisy high-throughput assay for protein and small-molecule binding; his team tamed it by putting dozens of barcodes on each molecule so they can catch proteins that bind the DNA tag instead of the drug. The headline result: their BELKA Kaggle competition served about 4 billion measurements collected in a basement to roughly 2,000 teams, and no model beat chance on a held-out chemical library. A second finding shows models can infer a molecule's chemist from its structure, and the author's identity predicts activity about as well as the structure itself, meaning public benchmarks are leaking human priors. Their in-house model Hermes, trained only on Leash data, keeps pace with Boltz on public benchmarks, and they caught it memorising protein nicknames instead of binding pockets, which forced them to engineer protein mutants at scale. The episode ends with the agent angle: an LLM sandbox where agents reason about structure and activity in plain English to find the best binders among 6.5M molecules on a budget of 100 picks, now released as the Minos preprint and open dataset, pitched as tens of thousands of lab-in-the-loop simulations for scientific RL.
