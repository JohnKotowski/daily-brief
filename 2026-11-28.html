Card skimmer found on Samsonite, Revolve and other stores came in through a marketing pixel and a cookie banner InfoHawk · HN 525It sent card numbers out over WebRTC, a channel the usual browser security rule does not cover.

InfoHawk reports that a skimmer was planted on Lascana, Samsonite, American Tourister, Revolve and FWRD in the week before Black Friday, and that all five were cleaned on Nov 24 and 25 after its reports. The attackers got in three ways: an altered Quorum marketing pixel, an edited setting in the Seers cookie-consent banner, and a changed file on Revolve's own content network. The loader ran only on mobile and touch checkouts. It sent card data out over a WebRTC data channel through Cloudflare's relay service, which a Content Security Policy connect-src rule does not restrict. InfoHawk saw its own test card taken only on Lascana and does not know how many shoppers were affected; Revolve says it "resolved the same day". The article links the campaign to the Nov 16 compromise of Optimizely, which reportedly reached the sites of 178 organisations.

Nature: rumours that AI labs hold attacks on public-key encryption; a preprint claims a faster discrete-log algorithm Nature · IACR ePrint · djbNothing is confirmed. The preprint depends on an unpublished OpenAI result, and proof checkers have bugs of their own.

Nature reports that several researchers have heard that leading labs found fast attacks on the mathematics behind public-key cryptography, with RSA factoring named in one rumour, and says "the rumours could not be confirmed." It quotes Scott Aaronson: "I'm virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that the AI labs are now sitting on one or more bombshells." Nature also notes results from Anthropic and OpenAI this week suggesting many related problems remain hard. Separately, three Zhejiang University authors posted a preprint claiming a deterministic algorithm for the discrete logarithm problem, in prime fields and on prime-order elliptic curves, that runs in subexponential time. Much of the key exchange on the internet rests on that problem being hard. The claim is conditional on a construction attributed to OpenAI from October, and the paper has not been peer reviewed.

A third piece bears on how far to trust any of this. Daniel J. Bernstein published a catalogue of soundness bugs in proof checkers, meaning bugs that could let a false statement pass: 20 in Isabelle and at least 22 in Lean, the checker behind most of this month's AI mathematics claims, and "in many cases, these bugs have been demonstrated to be exploitable." He recommends running proofs through more than one checker. Nothing here is confirmed, and nothing calls for action from you today. I read the free half of Nature's article, the preprint's abstract and most of Bernstein's page.
